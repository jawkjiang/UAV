\begin{abstract}
Real-time GPS spoofing detection for safety-critical UAV missions
raises two questions that conventional classification metrics cannot
answer: \emph{when} is a threat detected, and \emph{how often} do
false alarms disrupt operations.
We adopt three operational metrics---DR@$\Delta t$, ADD, and MTBFA
with event-level aggregation---and apply them to seven deep-learning
architectures spanning convolutional, recurrent, and attention
paradigms, trained on a public $3{,}000$-flight MATLAB UAV Toolbox
dataset with four parameterised attack families (Step, Drift, Delay,
Takeover) and evaluated with significance testing.
\textbf{(i) Calibration trade-off dominates.}
At the default threshold $p=0.5$, no architecture meets the
operational MTBFA budget; calibrated thresholding restores
MTBFA-feasibility for five of seven, while LSTM and GRU remain borderline,
and the DR@5\,s cost varies roughly five-fold across them.
Under the joint deployability criterion (DR@$5\,$s~$\geq 0.85$ \emph{and}
MTBFA~$\geq 0.10$\,h), however, CNN-LSTM is the only architecture that
remains feasible after calibration (calibrated DR@5\,s~$=0.880$,
MTBFA~$=0.178$\,h); the other four MTBFA-feasible architectures fall
below the DR@5\,s floor.
\textbf{(ii) Architecture--attack interactions are pronounced and
non-monotone.} TCN matches the field on temporally-structured attacks
but collapses on positional perturbations
(DR@5\,s~$=0.46$ on Drift vs $0.96$ on Takeover), so attack-averaged
metrics hide attack-specific failures.
\textbf{(iii) In-distribution ranking does not transfer, and naive
speed matching can hurt.}
CNN-LSTM, the in-distribution leader (DR@5\,s~$=0.96$), drops to
$0.20$ on real Holybro\,S500 hardware, and matching the simulator's
cruise speed to the target further degrades transfer
(CNN: $0.96 \to 0.00$; CNN-LSTM: $0.92 \to 0.08$), so this is an
insufficient sim-to-real remedy. We frame this as a zero-shot
cross-distribution stress test rather than a matched-platform deployment
validation: the real flights differ from the training distribution along
airframe, cruise speed, sensor noise, and attack mechanism.
Thresholds follow UAV power-line inspection risk constraints; code,
dataset, and trained checkpoints are released for reproducibility.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

With the widespread adoption of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) in power-system inspection and related applications, UAV navigation and positioning increasingly depend on global navigation satellite systems (GNSS/GPS) \cite{ref1}. In low-redundancy scenarios where external aids such as LiDAR or visual SLAM are unavailable, GNSS often becomes the primary (and sometimes the only) source for self-localization and waypoint tracking \cite{ref2}. This heavy reliance exposes UAVs to severe navigation-security threats-most notably \textbf{GNSS spoofing attacks} \cite{ref3}. In spoofing, an attacker transmits forged satellite-like signals that mislead the UAV into accepting falsified position information \cite{ref4}. During power-line inspection, manipulated navigation can cause trajectory deviation, boundary violations, and dangerously close approaches to high-voltage assets, potentially resulting in collisions, crashes, and mission failures \cite{ref5}. Such incidents can endanger both infrastructure and the UAV platform and may lead to substantial economic losses \cite{ref6}.

To address these threats, industry and academia have increasingly studied UAV GNSS spoofing detection and explored deep-learning-based solutions. Borhani-Darian \textit{et al.} proposed a detection approach combining deep neural networks with clustering and demonstrated strong performance across different SNR conditions \cite{ref7}. Sun \textit{et al.} built a hybrid architecture integrating PCA, CNN, and LSTM and reported 99.49\% accuracy for GPS spoofing detection \cite{ref8}. Badar \textit{et al.} proposed a CNN-BiLSTM hybrid model and improved detection on imbalanced datasets via feature selection, achieving 99.25\% accuracy \cite{ref9}. More recent works extend the architectural space: Alhoraibi \textit{et al.} detect UAV GPS spoofing using an adversarial machine-learning model \cite{ref34}, and Li \textit{et al.} propose Transformer-based real-time GNSS spoofing detection for small UAVs \cite{ref35}. Recent systematic literature reviews~\cite{ref15} catalogue further architectural and methodological variants in this rapidly growing area.

However, most existing work evaluates such detectors using conventional classification metrics such as \textbf{precision} and \textbf{recall}. While these static metrics capture offline classification quality, they are limited for practical deployment where \textbf{timeliness} and \textbf{false-alarm control} are critical. In other words, even a detector with high offline precision may be unsafe in real operations if it cannot raise alarms promptly or if it produces frequent false alarms.

The absence of an explicit time dimension prevents traditional metrics from quantifying dynamic properties such as response speed and false-alarm frequency. In intrusion detection systems (IDS), researchers have long recognized these limitations. Axelsson showed that IDS usability depends not only on detection rate but also on false-alarm rate; even 99\% detection becomes impractical with a 10\% false-alarm rate at scale \cite{ref10}. Sommer and Paxson further discussed the \textbf{base-rate fallacy}: in real environments where attacks are rare, even high-accuracy detectors can generate large numbers of false alarms due to the low base rate \cite{ref11}. These insights highlight how precision/recall can be misleading for imbalanced data, yet they still do not quantify detection delay.

In UAV navigation-security detection, evaluation remains dominated by conventional metrics. For example, Almadhor \textit{et al.} proposed the compact CTDNN-Spoof architecture and reported mainly accuracy and training time \cite{ref12}. A survey by Rehman Badar \textit{et al.} indicates that nearly all GNSS spoofing detection studies rely on precision and recall and severely lack quantification of time sensitivity, especially for false-alarm frequency and alert delay \cite{ref9}. Shafique \textit{et al.} reviewed more than 20 GNSS spoofing detection methods and found that almost all used accuracy/precision/recall; only three papers mentioned "detection time," and none provided a clear definition or systematic measurement \cite{ref13}.

Neglecting time factors can have serious operational consequences. Even if a detector is highly accurate offline, a long alarm delay may allow the UAV to drift hundreds of meters before detection-too late for safe intervention. Likewise, frequent false alarms can overwhelm operators and lead to \textbf{alarm fatigue} \cite{ref14}, causing alerts to be ignored and rendering the system ineffective, increasing the risk of accidents and economic loss. Therefore, relying solely on static metrics such as precision and recall is inadequate for real-time UAV navigation-security missions \cite{ref9}, \cite{ref15}.

Some real-time-focused domains have begun emphasizing detection speed. Panda and Guo proposed a Bayesian online changepoint detection (BOCPD) method that detects subtle spoofing-induced trajectory drift and significantly reduces recognition time \cite{ref16}. Dasgupta \textit{et al.} used reinforcement learning to detect GPS spoofing in autonomous navigation; their model can trigger timely alerts within maneuver intervals while maintaining high precision and recall \cite{ref17}. However, these works often define domain-specific temporal metrics and lack a general-purpose evaluation framework.

In time-series anomaly detection, Tatbul \textit{et al.} proposed \textbf{time-series-aware precision and recall}, incorporating temporal continuity of anomalies into evaluation \cite{ref18}. They argued that pointwise metrics overcount sustained anomalies: for instance, a 10-minute anomaly at 1 Hz marked continuously would produce 600 true positives, even though it should be counted as a single successfully detected event. Their event-level evaluation mitigates this distortion but mainly targets offline settings and does not model causality constraints or detection delay in online detection.

Motivated by these observations, this paper presents a comprehensive empirical
study of seven representative deep-learning UAV GPS spoofing detectors,
evaluated under both conventional and operational time-aware metrics
(DR@$\Delta t$, ADD, MTBFA with false-alarm event aggregation), with thresholds
grounded in UAV power-line inspection risk constraints.
The study addresses three open questions:
(1)~Is the operational deployability of simulator-trained detectors limited
by their default decision threshold, and how does the cost of recovering
deployability vary across architectures?
(2)~Do attack-averaged conventional metrics mask architecture-specific failure
modes that emerge only under attack-conditional evaluation?
(3)~Does in-distribution architecture ranking transfer to real-world
cross-distribution evaluation, and does naively matching the simulator's
nominal cruise speed to the target platform close the sim-to-real gap?
Concretely, our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Operational evaluation methodology grounded in deployment risk.}
  We adopt three runtime-aware metrics (DR@$\Delta t$, ADD, MTBFA) with
  false-alarm event aggregation aligning measurement with operator burden,
  and derive deployability thresholds (DR@5\,s~$\geq 0.85$, MTBFA~$\geq 0.10$\,h)
  from UAV power-line inspection risk constraints (5\,m/s cruise speed,
  GB\,26860-2011 safe-approach distance).
\item \textbf{Public 3\,000-flight MATLAB UAV Toolbox simulation dataset}
  with four parameterised GPS spoofing attack families (Step, Drift, Delay,
  Takeover), strictly isolated train/validation/test splits
  ($1{,}800$/$600$/$600$ flights), and reproducible per-flight seeding;
  released to support follow-on work.
\item \textbf{Multi-architecture benchmark with statistical rigor.}
  We benchmark seven representative deep-learning detector architectures
  (CNN, LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU, CNN-LSTM, TCN, Transformer) under both metric
  families, reporting mean~$\pm$~95\% CI ($1{,}000$-iteration percentile
  bootstrap~\cite{ref46}) over $5$ random seeds with
  Bonferroni-corrected~\cite{ref45} pairwise tests. We additionally release per-attack-type
  and per-distribution evaluations, a controlled platform-matching experiment,
  and a full validation-calibrated threshold sweep.
\item \textbf{Three deployment-relevant empirical findings} (\S\ref{sec:casestudy}):
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Calibration trade-off dominates deployment performance.}
      No architecture meets the operational MTBFA budget at the default
      decision threshold; validation-calibrated thresholding restores
      MTBFA-feasibility with substantial margin for five of the seven
      (CNN, BiLSTM, CNN-LSTM, TCN, Transformer); LSTM and GRU remain
      borderline at the swept-threshold boundary. Among the feasible
      architectures, the DR@5\,s cost of calibration varies by a factor
      of five (CNN-LSTM: $-0.08$; Transformer: $-0.43$). The shape of
      the calibration curve is itself an architectural fingerprint that
      conventional metrics cannot surface.
    \item \emph{Architecture--attack interaction.} TCN matches the field on
      temporally-structured attacks (DR@5\,s $=0.96$ on Takeover) but collapses
      on positional perturbations (Drift DR@5\,s $=0.46$), an asymmetry
      invisible to attack-averaged metrics.
    \item \emph{Cross-distribution non-preservation, with controlled-experiment
      evidence.} CNN-LSTM, the in-distribution leader (DR@5\,s $=0.96$), drops
      to $0.20$ on real Holybro\,S500 hardware data; a controlled
      $\{5\text{ m/s},\,20\text{ m/s matched}\}$ training-regime comparison
      further shows that nominally matching the simulator's cruise speed to
      the target platform's cruise speed degrades cross-distribution DR@5\,s
      ($0.36$--$0.96$ across architectures), contradicting the standard
      sim-to-real-matching intuition.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II defines the task and problem setup. Section III formalises the operational evaluation metrics adopted in this study. Section IV describes the dataset, attack injection methodology, detector architectures, and evaluation protocol. Section V reports the benchmark results, attack-architecture interaction analysis, calibration trade-off, cross-distribution evaluation, and controlled platform-matching experiment. Section VI discusses limitations and concludes.

\section{Problem Setup}

\subsection{Online Detection Task and Scope}

We study UAV navigation security for waypoint-based power-line inspection missions. Under low-redundancy navigation conditions without LiDAR or visual SLAM, the UAV primarily relies on GNSS/GPS observations and onboard inertial/kinematic states (position, velocity, acceleration) to track its trajectory \cite{ref19}. An attacker can spoof GNSS observations so that the ground station and flight controller receive location information that deviates from the true motion state, causing trajectory drift, boundary violations near critical infrastructure, or mission failure \cite{ref20}.

Our core task is: given a time-ordered onboard navigation state sequence \(\{\mathbf{x}_t\}\) (feature dimension \(d\)), train an online binary detector \(f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \{0,1\}\) that outputs at each decision time whether the system is under spoofing attack, i.e., \(\hat{y}_{\tau_k} \in \{0,1\}\). The operational goal involves two coupled dimensions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Timeliness}: once an attack starts, the system should trigger an alert as close as possible to the attack onset so that mitigation can be taken promptly (e.g., switching navigation sources, emergency return-to-home).
\item \textbf{False-alarm control}: during normal flight, false alarms should be minimized to reduce operational burden.
\end{itemize}

To make the notion of "online" reproducible in experiments, we define an attack as an event with a clear onset: the attack starts at time \(t_s\) and subsequently influences GNSS observations (duration is specified by the injection model; see Section IV-B). The ground-truth label can be expressed as

\begin{equation}
 y(t) = \mathbb{I}(t \ge t_s),
\end{equation}

where \(\mathbb{I}(\cdot)\) is the indicator function. Under this definition, key detector behavior is characterized by the \textbf{first alarm time} and the \textbf{detection delay}.

\subsection{Causality and Decision-Time Semantics}

Since the prediction made at time \(\tau_k\) can only depend on observations received up to \(\tau_k\) (and not on any future information), we introduce \textbf{Discrete Decision Times} to formalize causality.

We model online detection as producing predictions at a sequence of discrete times \(\{\tau_k\}_{k=1}^K\), where each \(\tau_k\) corresponds to a decision action. At decision time \(\tau_k\), the detector outputs a binary decision \(\hat{y}_{\tau_k}\) based only on observations up to \(\tau_k\).

Let the detector be a function \(f: \mathcal{X} \to \{0,1\}\) over an input space \(\mathcal{X}\) (e.g., fixed-length windows, variable-length sequences). At decision time \(\tau_k\), the detector receives input \(\mathbf{z}_k\) constructed from history and outputs

\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_{\tau_k} = f(\mathbf{z}_k).
\end{equation}

Define the first detection time (first positive after attack onset) and the attack onset time; then the detection delay is computed accordingly. A causal illustration of online detection is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:causality}.

The presentation follows the structure of related online-detection

causality and time-budget constructs needed for the safety-critical UAV

\subsection{Discrete-Time Formulation of Online Detection}

We formalize the output of an online detector as a discrete-time sequence:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D} = \{(\tau_k, \hat{y}_{\tau_k})\}_{k=1}^K,
\end{equation}

where \(\tau_k\) is the timestamp of the \(k\)-th decision and \(\hat{y}_{\tau_k}\) is the corresponding binary decision. For notational convenience, we also write \(\hat{y}_k\triangleq \hat{y}_{\tau_k}\) when the decision time is clear from context. This representation has three key properties:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Temporal order}: \(\tau_1 < \tau_2 < \cdots < \tau_K\).
\item \textbf{Causality}: each decision depends only on observations up to \(\tau_k\).
\item \textbf{Completeness}: the sequence covers the full evaluation horizon; any time \(t\) can be mapped to the nearest decision time.
\end{itemize}

Any detector that outputs timestamped decisions can be evaluated under this framework.

Given attack onset \(t_s\) (from injection metadata), the ground-truth label at decision time \(\tau_k\) is

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{G} = \{(\tau_k, y_k)\}_{k=1}^K, \quad y_k = \mathbb{I}(\tau_k \ge t_s).
\end{equation}

Note that ground truth is defined \textbf{at decision times}: at \(\tau_k\) the system answers "is the system under attack now," not "was there an attack at any time in the past interval."

\subsection{Event-Based Ground Truth and Detection Semantics}

Traditional classification often assumes i.i.d. samples and ignores temporal dependence \cite{ref21}. In online detection, spoofing is naturally an \textbf{event}: it starts at \(t_s\) and persists until flight termination or mitigation.

We represent an attack as a tuple:

\begin{equation}
 e = (t_s, t_e, type),
\end{equation}

where \(t_s\) is onset, \(t_e\) is end time (in this work, attacks persist to flight end), and \(type\) denotes the attack type (e.g., Step, Drift).

\subsubsection{First Detection Time and Detection Delay}

Given predictions \(\mathcal{D}\) and attack onset \(t_s\), the \textbf{first detection time} is

\begin{equation}
 t_d = \min \{\tau_k : \tau_k \ge t_s,\ \hat{y}_{\tau_k}=1\}.
\end{equation}

If no such \(\tau_k\) exists (attack not detected), let \(t_d = +\infty\). We count only positives after \(t_s\) to avoid treating pre-attack false positives as "early detection."

The \textbf{detection delay} is

\begin{equation}
\Delta t = t_d - t_s.
\end{equation}

This directly measures response speed; smaller \(\Delta t\) indicates faster detection. We use the \textbf{first} detection time since operational response requires only the first alarm to initiate mitigation.

\subsubsection{False-Alarm Event Aggregation}

In window-level evaluation, each normal window mislabeled as attack is counted as one false positive. Operationally, however, consecutive false-positive windows typically correspond to a \textbf{single false-alarm event}-the operator/system handles one alarm rather than many.

We thus define \textbf{false-alarm event aggregation}:

\textbf{Definition (False-alarm event).} During normal flight (\(y_{\tau_k}=0\)), if there exists a consecutive sequence of decision times with \(\hat{y}_{\tau_k}=1\), these consecutive positives constitute one false-alarm event.

Formally, the false-alarm event set \(\mathcal{F}\) can be constructed by the following procedure:

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Input:} prediction sequence $\mathcal{D}=\{(\tau_k,\hat{y}_k)\}_{k=1}^K$, ground-truth sequence $\mathcal{G}=\{(\tau_k,y_k)\}_{k=1}^K$
\STATE \textbf{Output:} false-alarm event set $\mathcal{F}$
\STATE $\mathcal{F} \leftarrow \emptyset$
\STATE $in\_fp\_event \leftarrow \mathrm{False}$
\FOR{$k=1$ to $K$}
\IF{$y_k=0$ and $\hat{y}_k=1$}
\IF{not $in\_fp\_event$}
\STATE create a new event $e=(\tau_k,\tau_k)$
\STATE $in\_fp\_event \leftarrow \mathrm{True}$
\ELSE
\STATE update the event end time $e.t_{\mathrm{end}} \leftarrow \tau_k$
\ENDIF
\ELSE
\IF{$in\_fp\_event$}
\STATE add $e$ to $\mathcal{F}$
\STATE $in\_fp\_event \leftarrow \mathrm{False}$
\ENDIF
\ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\IF{$in\_fp\_event$}
\STATE add $e$ to $\mathcal{F}$
\ENDIF
\STATE \textbf{return} $\mathcal{F}$
\end{algorithmic}

If the detector produces a sustained false alarm over a time interval, an operator (or a downstream decision module) typically needs to handle only one alarm at the event onset rather than react at every time step. Therefore, counting aggregated false-alarm events makes the measured false-alarm frequency closer to the actual operational burden.

\subsection{Time-Aware Evaluation Metrics}

Based on the above event-based modeling, we adopt and operationalise three time-aware metrics capturing detection speed and false-alarm frequency. The metrics themselves build on prior event-aware evaluation literature (see \S\ref{sec:positioning}); our contribution is their systematisation, time-budgeted instantiation, and mission-grounded threshold derivation, not the originality of any individual metric.

\subsubsection{Detection Rate at Time Threshold (DR@$\Delta t$)}

Given a time threshold \(\Delta t\), \textbf{DR@Δt} is the fraction of attacks detected within \(\Delta t\) after the attack onset.

For \(N\) attack events with delays \(\{\Delta t_i\}\) (undetected attacks have \(\Delta t_i=+\infty\)):

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{DR@\Delta t} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{I}(\Delta t_i \le \Delta t).
\end{equation}

For example, DR@5s = 0.98 means 98\% of attacks are detected within 5 seconds.

Compared with the traditional recall metric, recall answers whether an attack is eventually detected, i.e., whether the detection delay is finite ($\Delta t_i < \infty$), and it does not consider how quickly the alarm is triggered. In contrast, DR@$\Delta t$ evaluates whether an attack is detected within a prescribed time budget, i.e., whether $\Delta t_i \le \Delta t$, and therefore explicitly introduces a time constraint. For example, two detectors may achieve the same recall (both equal to $1.0$, meaning that all attacks are eventually detected), yet their DR@$5\,\mathrm{s}$ can be $0.95$ and $0.70$, respectively---the former detects $95\%$ of attacks within $5\,\mathrm{s}$, while the latter detects only $70\%$, indicating a substantial difference in runtime performance.

\subsubsection{Average Detection Delay (ADD)}

For all successfully detected attacks, \textbf{ADD} is the mean detection delay:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ADD} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_d|}\sum_{i\in \mathcal{I}_d} \Delta t_i,
\end{equation}

where \(\mathcal{I}\_d\) is the set of detected attacks.

ADD provides a single-number summary for comparing response speed across models. In practice, ADD is useful for ranking, while a DR@$\Delta t$ curve over multiple thresholds reveals the distribution of detection speed.

\subsubsection{Mean Time Between False Alarms (MTBFA)}

Let \(T_n\) be the total normal-flight time (seconds) and \(|\mathcal{F}|\) be the number of false-alarm events (after aggregation). Then

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MTBFA} = \frac{T_n/3600}{|\mathcal{F}|}\ \text{(hours)}.
\end{equation}

MTBFA directly maps to operational burden: higher MTBFA means fewer false alarms and better reliability.

Unlike the traditional precision metric, which is defined at the window level as

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Precision} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}},
\end{equation}

where TP and FP are counts of true-positive and false-positive windows, MTBFA directly answers an operational question: how often false alarms occur over time. This difference arises from three factors:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Different counting units}: the denominator of precision is the number of predicted-positive windows, whereas the denominator of MTBFA is the number of false-alarm \emph{events}.
\item \textbf{Missing time dimension in precision}: precision is a dimensionless ratio, while MTBFA is a time-based frequency (e.g., hours per false-alarm event).
\item \textbf{Aggregation effect}: 100 consecutive false-positive windows contribute 100 FP to precision, but they form only one false-alarm event in MTBFA after event aggregation.
\end{itemize}

To illustrate, consider two detectors A and B evaluated over the same normal-flight duration; the example is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:precision-mtbfa-example}.

Detector A has higher precision (0.980 $>$ 0.960) but lower MTBFA (0.2 h $<$ 0.4 h). The reason is that A's 100 FP windows aggregate into 10 events (about 10 consecutive windows per event on average), whereas B's 200 FP windows aggregate into only 5 events (about 40 consecutive windows per event). From an operational perspective, detector B triggers fewer distinct alarms, so its MTBFA is higher. This paradox highlights a key limitation of conventional metrics: high precision does not guarantee low false-alarm frequency, and the time-domain aggregation effect must be modeled explicitly.

\subsection{Window-Based Instantiation of Decision Times}

The framework defined in Sections IV-A to IV-C specifies the time-aware evaluation system at an abstract level, but it does not prescribe how the decision times $\{\tau_k\}$ should be generated. This subsection presents the sliding-window instantiation adopted in this paper as a reproducible implementation of the framework.

Given a continuously sampled observation sequence $\{\mathbf{x}(t_i)\}_{i=1}^{M}$ with sampling times $t_1<t_2<\cdots<t_M$, we slice the sequence using a fixed window length $L$ (window size) and a fixed step size $S$:

\begin{equation}
w_k = \{\mathbf{x}(t_i)\mid i\in[i_k,\, i_k+L-1]\},\quad i_k = 1+(k-1)\cdot S.
\end{equation}

Each window $w_k$ covers the time interval $[t_{i_k},\,t_{i_k+L-1}]$ and contains $L$ consecutive samples. We define the decision time of $w_k$ as its end timestamp:

\begin{equation}
\tau_k = t_{i_k+L-1} = t_{\mathrm{end}}^{(w_k)}.
\end{equation}

At time $\tau_k$, all observations in $w_k$ have arrived. The detector outputs the online prediction $\hat{y}_k=f(w_k)$ using only data with $t\le \tau_k$, which satisfies the causality constraint in Section III-B.

Consistent with the decision-time semantics, we use end-point labeling to assign the ground-truth label of each window:

\begin{equation}
y_k = y\bigl(t_{\mathrm{end}}^{(w_k)}\bigr)=\mathbb{I}\bigl(t_{\mathrm{end}}^{(w_k)}\ge t_s\bigr).
\end{equation}

If the window end time has entered the attack phase ($t_{\mathrm{end}}^{(w_k)}\ge t_s$), then $y_k=1$; otherwise ($t_{\mathrm{end}}^{(w_k)}<t_s$), $y_k=0$.

Under this windowed instantiation, the first detection time $\tau_{\mathrm{det}}$ is the end timestamp of the first window predicted as positive after attack onset:

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{det}} = t_{\mathrm{end}}^{(w_{k^*})},\quad k^* = \min\{k\mid \hat{y}_k=1\wedge \tau_k\ge t_s\}.
\end{equation}

The detection delay is

\begin{equation}
\Delta t_{\mathrm{det}} = \tau_{\mathrm{det}}-t_s = t_{\mathrm{end}}^{(w_{k^*})}-t_s.
\end{equation}

Example: suppose the attack onset is $t_s=12.34\,\mathrm{s}$ (from injection metadata). If window $w_5$ covers $[10.0,\,15.0]$ s and ends at $t_{\mathrm{end}}^{(w_5)}=15.0\,\mathrm{s}$, and if $w_5$ is the first predicted-positive window, then $\tau_{\mathrm{det}}=15.0\,\mathrm{s}$ and $\Delta t_{\mathrm{det}}=15.0-12.34=2.66\,\mathrm{s}$.

Note that $t_s$ is recorded with sample-level precision during injection, while $\tau_k$ is constrained by the window step size $S$. Therefore, the computed delay precisely reflects the real elapsed time between attack onset and the system's first response. The sliding-window instantiation process is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:windowing}.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_iv_1_windowing.pdf}
\caption{Sliding-window instantiation and decision-time semantics.}
\label{fig:windowing}
\end{figure}

Importantly, the proposed framework does not depend on the sliding-window data structure. Sections IV-A to IV-C only require the detector to output a timestamped decision sequence $\{(\tau_k,\hat{y}_k)\}$, and do not impose any constraint on how $\tau_k$ is generated.

We adopt sliding windows primarily for deep-model compatibility, since fixed-length windows are a standard input format for architectures such as CNNs, LSTMs, and Transformers. Nevertheless, the framework remains open to other instantiation strategies, for example:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Variable-length sequences}: feed an entire flight segment to an RNN and output a decision at each time step (e.g., $\tau_k=t_k$).
\item \textbf{Event-triggered decisions}: trigger decisions only when an observation appears abnormal, yielding a non-uniformly sampled set of decision times $\{\tau_k\}$.
\item \textbf{Multi-scale windows}: use multiple window lengths in parallel and fuse information across time scales.
\end{itemize}

As long as an instantiation produces a causal timestamped decision sequence, it can be evaluated under the proposed time-aware framework.

\textbf{Tatbul et al.'s range-based metrics}~\cite{ref18} score anomaly
\emph{segments} against predicted ranges in a post-hoc, offline manner,
rewarding any detection within a labelled anomaly range equally regardless
of when the first detection occurs.
Our DR@$\Delta t$ instead scores only the \emph{first detection event}
within a specified time budget, enforcing causality and response-time
accountability.

\textbf{The Numenta Anomaly Benchmark (NAB) score}~\cite{ref57}
applies a window-weighted reward that discounts late detections and
penalises false positives via score penalties. While NAB is causal, it
does not expose the time-to-first-detection as an interpretable parameter,
making it difficult to translate a score into a concrete operational
response-time budget.

\textbf{Classical IDS false-alarm metrics} (FAR, FPR) quantify false
positives as a fraction of normal-class windows~\cite{ref10}. These metrics
treat each window as independent and therefore count the same persistent
alarm multiple times. Our MTBFA instead aggregates consecutive
false-positive windows into a single event and expresses the rate in
operational time (hours), a unit directly interpretable by flight operators.

Our contribution is therefore the systematisation of
(i)~explicit time-budgeted detection-rate (DR@$\Delta t$) with
first-detection causality;
(ii)~event-level false-alarm aggregation expressed in operational time
units (MTBFA);
(iii)~deployment-grounded threshold derivation tied to mission-specific
risk constraints (\S\ref{sec:deployability_standard});
and (iv)~a UAV GNSS-spoofing-detection case study that exercises this
operational evaluation on seven representative architectures across
multiple distributions (Sections~\ref{sec:cross_domain}--\ref{sec:alfa-controlled}).

Four mission profiles are generated to model power-line inspection tasks:

The sensor pipeline combines:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GPS:} \texttt{gpsSensor} with horizontal noise $\sigma_{xy}=\SI{1.5}{\metre}$

\subsection{Threat Model and Attack Injection}
\label{sec:threat_model}

Since the MATLAB UAV Toolbox simulation generates normal flights by default, we adopt \textbf{injection-based attack generation}: while preserving mission context and flight dynamics, we apply parameterized perturbations to GPS position observations to create attacked flights with explicit onset and duration semantics.

We implement four major attack families (Step, Drift, Delay, Takeover) and a direction-generation scheme shared across attacks.
Each family has a documented real-world analogue: the 2011 RQ-170 Sentinel capture
(attributed to GPS spoofing causing the UAV to believe it was at a landing site with
zero altitude) exemplifies a combined Step/Takeover scenario \cite{ref24};
the 2013 Humphreys yacht-hijacking demonstration \cite{ref49} used a smooth Drift
pattern to steer the vessel off course without alerting the crew;
the 2016 Black Sea maritime spoofing cluster \cite{ref50} caused vessels to report
positions kilometres inland, consistent with abrupt Step offsets.

\subsubsection{Step Attack (abrupt position jump)}

In a step attack, the position observation undergoes an instantaneous offset at time $t_s$, emulating sudden signal takeover or a position jump \cite{ref24}. Let the UAV's original horizontal position be $\mathbf{p}(t)=[p_x(t),\,p_y(t)]^\top$ and the injected position be $\mathbf{p}'(t)$. Let $M\in\{5,15,30\}$ (meters) denote the offset magnitude and $\hat{\mathbf{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^2$ be a unit direction vector. Then

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}'(t)=
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{p}(t), & t<t_s,\\
\mathbf{p}(t)+M\hat{\mathbf{d}}, & t\ge t_s.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

To emulate short-term kinematic disturbances induced by the abrupt jump, we superimpose a transient velocity term:

\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathbf{v}(t)=
\begin{cases}
 v_{\max}\hat{\mathbf{d}}\exp\bigl(-(t-t_s)/\tau\bigr), & t_s\le t\le t_s+T_{\mathrm{dur}},\\
 \mathbf{0}, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $v_{\max}\in[0.5,2.0]$ m/s, $\tau=1.0$ s, and $T_{\mathrm{dur}}=5.0$ s. The acceleration transient is computed by finite differences:

\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathbf{a}(t_i)=\frac{\Delta\mathbf{v}(t_i)-\Delta\mathbf{v}(t_{i-1})}{\Delta t_i}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Drift Attack (gradually increasing offset)}

In a drift attack, the position offset accumulates over time, representing slow steering and progressive spoofing \cite{ref16}:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}'(t)=\mathbf{p}(t)+M\,g(t)\,\hat{\mathbf{d}},
\end{equation}

where $T_{\mathrm{drift}}\in\{5,10,20\}$ s is the drift duration and $g(t)$ is a growth function

\begin{equation}
g(t)=
\begin{cases}
0, & t<t_s,\\
(t-t_s)/T_{\mathrm{drift}}, & t_s\le t\le t_s+T_{\mathrm{drift}},\\
1, & t>t_s+T_{\mathrm{drift}}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Similarly to the step attack, we add the same velocity transient $\Delta\mathbf{v}(t)$ to model short-term kinematic perturbations around the onset.

\subsubsection{Delay Attack (systematic time lag)}

In a delay attack, the position observation is systematically lagged (replay / time bias), reflecting a trajectory shift on the time axis \cite{ref25}:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}'(t)=
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{p}(t), & t<t_s,\\
\mathbf{p}(t-\delta), & t\ge t_s,
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $\delta\in\{1.0,3.0,5.0\}$ s is the delay. In implementation, we use linear interpolation:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}'(t)=\mathrm{interp}\bigl(\mathbf{p},\,t-\delta\bigr),
\end{equation}

and apply boundary handling by clamping to $\mathbf{p}(t_{\min})$ when $t-\delta<t_{\min}$.

\subsubsection{Takeover Attack (smooth transition to a forged trajectory)}

In a takeover attack, the spoofed trajectory is smoothly pulled toward a forged reference trajectory, emulating gradual control of the navigation system \cite{ref26}. We first define a reference (target forged) trajectory

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)=\mathbf{p}(t)+M\,g(t)\,\hat{\mathbf{d}},
\end{equation}

where $g(t)$ can be chosen as a step, ramp, or sigmoid (the ramp form above is used by default). We then apply a first-order tracking dynamics in discrete time:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}'[i]=\mathbf{p}'[i-1]+\alpha\,\bigl(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{ref}}[i]-\mathbf{p}'[i-1]\bigr),
\end{equation}

where $\alpha\in\{0.3,0.5,0.7\}$ is a constant tracking gain.

\subsubsection{Direction patterns (generation of $\hat{\mathbf{d}}$)}

The direction vector $\hat{\mathbf{d}}$ is generated according to one of the following patterns (Table~\ref{tab:direction-patterns}).

\subsubsection{Injection constraints}

\textbf{Temporal buffer.} To avoid injecting attacks near takeoff and landing (where dynamics are complex and may confound evaluation), we require

\begin{equation}
t_s\in[t_{\min}+2.0,\,t_{\max}-2.0]\ \text{(seconds)},
\end{equation}

where $t_{\min}$ and $t_{\max}$ are the flight start and end times.

\textbf{Injection ratios.} The attacked-flight ratio is set to 40\% in all three splits (train, validation, test). Per-flight randomness is determined deterministically by the flight ID, so observed split-level ratios are 41\%/40\%/38\% (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}). The 40\% target ensures sufficient samples per attack family in every split for both training-distribution coverage and statistically meaningful per-attack-type evaluation in \S\ref{sec:per-attack}.

\textbf{Attack-type distribution.} The four attack families (Step, Drift, Delay, Takeover) are uniformly distributed within each split so that models are exposed to diverse threats during both training and testing.

\subsection{Windowing and Ground-Truth Labeling}

After generating attacked flights, we apply sliding windows to convert continuous sequences into fixed-length inputs compatible with deep models. The window generation rule is as follows: given a flight sequence $\{\mathbf{x}(t_i)\}_{i=1}^{M}$, the $k$-th window is

\begin{equation}
w_k = \{\mathbf{x}(t_i)\mid i\in[i_k,\, i_k+L-1]\},\quad i_k = 1+(k-1)\cdot S.
\end{equation}

The total number of windows is

\begin{equation}
K = \left\lfloor\frac{M-L}{S}\right\rfloor + 1.
\end{equation}

Window configuration is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:window-config}.
The window length $L=50$ samples is chosen because one window must span the
DR@5\,s detection budget at the simulation rate $f_s = 10$\,Hz, that is,
$L = \Delta t \cdot f_s = 5\,\text{s} \times 10\,\text{Hz} = 50$ samples.
The step size $S=5$ samples preserves $90\%$ overlap between successive windows,
so the detector issues a fresh decision every $0.5$\,s.
The sensitivity of the framework's conclusions to the choice of $(L, S)$ is
reported in \S\ref{sec:ablation}.

\subsection{Deployment Safety Standard}
\label{sec:deployability_standard}

\subsubsection{Mission-specific derivation of the DR@5\,s screening threshold}

The DR@$5\,\text{s}$ threshold used in this paper is a \emph{mission-specific
operational screening criterion} for power-line inspection, not a universal
safety guarantee. Different missions, vehicles, or operating environments
require re-derivation; we report the full DR@$\{1, 5, 10, 15\}\,\text{s}$
curves in Table~\ref{tab:overall-timeaware} to support such re-derivation.

\paragraph{Project assumptions.}
For the case study reported here, we make the following project-level
engineering assumptions:
(i)~the UAV cruises at $v=5$\,m/s, the typical inspection speed for tower
and conductor proximity surveys~\cite{ref29};
(ii)~as a worst case, the spoofing-induced drift is assumed parallel to the
shortest line to the nearest energised conductor (which most directly
reduces standoff);
(iii)~the planned standoff at the moment of attack onset is
$d_0 = 35\,\text{m}$, a project-specified value compatible with industrial
inspection guidelines for $500\,\text{kV}$ structures (precise value depends
on operator, regulator, wind margin, and platform; we report this as a
mission assumption rather than a regulatory minimum);
(iv)~upon detection, mitigation requires switching to inertial-only
dead-reckoning and initiating a vertical-climb evasive manoeuvre, with a
combined latency budget of $t_{\text{mit}} \approx 1\,\text{s}$ (typical
multirotor actuator-command-to-airframe-response latency, taken as a
working assumption; site-specific platforms may require larger margins);
(v)~the regulatory \emph{minimum} safe approach distance to a
$\SI{500}{\kV}$ transmission line is $d_{\min}=\SI{5}{\metre}$ under
\emph{GB\,26860-2011}~\cite{ref30}.

\paragraph{Drift-budget computation.}
At cruise speed, drift over a detection budget $\Delta t$ is
$\Delta x = v \cdot \Delta t$. Under the above assumptions the deployable
detection budget must satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \Delta x + v \cdot t_{\text{mit}} + d_{\min} \leq d_{0},
\end{equation}
yielding $\Delta t \leq (d_0 - d_{\min} - v \cdot t_{\text{mit}})/v
= (35-5-5)/5 = 5\,\text{s}$.

\paragraph{Recall criterion.}
We adopt DR@$5\,\text{s} \geq 0.85$ as the operational screening criterion
for this case study. This corresponds to requiring that no more than $15\%$
of attacks remain undetected by the end of the $5$-second abort budget; we
emphasise that this is a coarse aggregate proxy rather than a direct bound on
boundary-violation probability, which additionally depends on attack
direction, drift dynamics, controller response, environmental disturbance,
and operator policy. The $0.85$ value is a project-level engineering
threshold; alternative choices (e.g., $0.90$ for stricter $500$\,kV
operation, $0.75$ for $35\,\text{kV}$ distribution) follow from the same
derivation with adjusted $d_0$ or $t_{\text{mit}}$. The framework is
reported at multiple $\Delta t$ values explicitly to support re-derivation
under different mission profiles. Alarm-management guidance from
medical and process-control domains~\cite{ref32} is treated as
supporting analogy rather than primary basis.

\subsubsection{Risk-based derivation of MTBFA threshold}

A false alarm requires the remote pilot to verify sensor data and potentially
abort the current mission leg.
A pilot can reasonably absorb approximately 10 nuisance alarms per
$\SI{1}{\hour}$ inspection tour without significant workload increase
\cite{ref32,ref10}, giving a minimum tolerable $\text{MTBFA} \geq 0.1\,\text{h}$.

\subsubsection{Deployability verdict}

A model passes the time-aware standard if and only if:
\begin{equation}
  \text{DR@5s} \geq 0.85 \quad \text{and} \quad \text{MTBFA} \geq 0.1\,\text{h}.
\end{equation}
For completeness we also report DR at $\{1,10,15\}\,\text{s}$ and
full CI bounds so practitioners can apply alternative thresholds.

\subsection{Deep Dive: Detection Speed and False-Alarm Frequency}

This section provides a mechanistic analysis of detection speed and false-alarm frequency, revealing operational characteristics captured by time-aware metrics that traditional metrics cannot express.

\subsubsection{Detection speed analysis}

The DR@$\Delta t$ curve (as a function of threshold $\Delta t$) reveals information
that conventional recall cannot: as $\Delta t \to \infty$ all models approach 100\%
detection rate and become indistinguishable, yet within the critical 5-second
response window substantial inter-model differences emerge (see Fig.~\ref{fig:dr-curves}
and Table~\ref{tab:overall-timeaware}).
Traditional recall answers ``how many are eventually detected,''
while DR@$\Delta t$ answers ``how many are detected under different time constraints''---
this temporal distribution information cannot be captured by traditional metrics
regardless of threshold adjustment.

Most models reach detection-rate saturation ($>$98\%) around 10 seconds.
Models with higher ADD suffer from detection-delay risk: even if their
long-run recall is high, a substantial fraction of attacks are not flagged
within the safety window.
The DR@$\Delta t$ curves of all detectors are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:dr-curves}.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_c1_dr_at_delta_t_curves.pdf}
\caption{DR@$\Delta t$ curves of different detectors, illustrating differences in detection timeliness under varying response-time thresholds.}
\label{fig:dr-curves}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{False-alarm frequency analysis}

Precision and MTBFA can exhibit a non-monotonic relationship: a model with
\emph{higher} precision can have \emph{lower} MTBFA than a model with lower precision.
This occurs when the higher-precision model produces fewer total false-positive
windows that are nonetheless dispersed across time, forming many independent events;
whereas the lower-precision model clusters its false-positive windows consecutively
so they aggregate into fewer events.
This directly demonstrates that precision and MTBFA measure fundamentally different
quantities---precision cannot answer the operational question ``how often do false alarms
occur.''
The paradox is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:precision-mtbfa-paradox}.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_b3_precision_mtbfa_paradox.pdf}
\caption{Illustration of the precision--MTBFA paradox caused by temporal aggregation effects.}
\label{fig:precision-mtbfa-paradox}
\end{figure}

The aggregation effect is visible across every architecture in our case study.
Fig.~\ref{fig:fp-windows-vs-events} contrasts the raw count of false-positive
windows against the count of aggregated false-alarm events for each detector on
the test split. All seven detectors collapse roughly $4$--$7$ consecutive
false-positive windows into a single operational alarm event. The empirical
aggregation ratio (windows per event) is $4.19$ for CNN, $5.02$ for CNN-LSTM,
$5.72$ for GRU, $6.34$ for LSTM, $6.35$ for BiLSTM, $6.48$ for Transformer, and
$6.87$ for TCN. These per-architecture ratios reveal why window-level precision
is a poor proxy for the alarm rate that an operator actually experiences.
The two extremes illustrate this point concretely: TCN produces the fewest raw
false-positive windows ($\approx 1131$), yet its false positives are the most
temporally clustered, whereas Transformer produces the most raw false-positive
windows ($\approx 3725$), and these are dispersed into the largest number of
distinct events. Two detectors with similar precision can therefore differ by
an order of magnitude in MTBFA, depending on how their false positives
distribute over time.

\end{figure}

Overall, the models that score highest on both DR@5s and MTBFA represent the best

DR@5s carry detection-delay risk, while models with adequate DR@5s but low MTBFA
carry false-alarm risk.

\label{sec:threshold-sensitivity}

To demonstrate that the framework's conclusions are not artifacts of the specific
DR@5s and MTBFA thresholds, we evaluate deployability decisions across a grid of
threshold combinations: DR time horizon $\Delta t \in \{1, 5, 10, 15\}\,\mathrm{s}$
and MTBFA threshold $\tau_{\mathrm{FA}} \in \{0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20\}\,\mathrm{h}$.

Key observations:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Risk-identification is robust across thresholds.}
Models that fail under the primary thresholds (DR@5s $\ge$ 85\%, MTBFA $\ge$ 0.1\,h)
continue to fail when thresholds are relaxed, indicating that their deficiencies are
systematic rather than threshold-sensitive.

\item \textbf{Relative rankings are stable.}

The framework's core value lies in introducing the time dimension, not in any

apply; for more lenient scenarios (long-range transit, moderate payload) thresholds
may be relaxed while retaining the same evaluation methodology.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Threshold sensitivity}

To test that deployability decisions are not artefacts of the specific
DR@$\Delta t$ time horizon and MTBFA tolerance, we evaluate every architecture
over a grid of $\Delta t \in \{1, 5, 10, 15\}\,\text{s}$ and
MTBFA threshold $\tau_{\mathrm{FA}} \in \{0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20\}\,\text{h}$.
At the default decision threshold $p=0.5$, the empirical MTBFA of every
architecture falls in the range $0.016$--$0.043$\,h
(test-set cumulative normal time is approximately 7\,h):
no architecture satisfies MTBFA~$\ge 0.1$\,h regardless of the chosen DR horizon.

This is not a measurement-scale issue---7\,h of cumulative normal time
is sufficient to estimate MTBFA at the $0.1$\,h scale---but a deployment-engineering
finding: \emph{at the default operating threshold, none of the architectures
is deployable}. Detectors trained on the simulated distribution
generate too many isolated low-confidence positives in the cross-window
sense to satisfy the operator workload budget.

\paragraph{Validation-calibrated operating point.}
To test whether the failure is fundamental or a calibration artefact, we
re-evaluate every (architecture, seed) under a validation-set-calibrated
decision threshold. The procedure follows the classical ROC operating-point
selection methodology~\cite{ref53}, adapted to a deployability constraint
in the spirit of recent threshold-tuning practice for highly imbalanced and
safety-critical classification~\cite{ref55}; because the underlying
detector logits are not necessarily well-calibrated probabilities, the
sweep also functions as a non-parametric probability-calibration step
analogous to post-hoc calibration techniques~\cite{ref54}.
For each model we sweep
$p \in [0.50, 0.99]$ in steps of $0.01$, select the smallest $p^\star$ that
yields MTBFA$(\text{val})\ge 0.10$\,h, and report DR@5\,s, MTBFA on the
held-out test set under that $p^\star$.
Table~\ref{tab:calibrated-threshold} summarises the result.

The calibrated experiment yields four findings.

\textbf{(a) MTBFA-feasibility splits the architecture set into a robust majority
and a marginal minority.}
Five of the seven architectures (CNN, BiLSTM, CNN-LSTM, TCN, Transformer)
reach the operational MTBFA target $\geq 0.10$\,h with substantial margin
at calibrated operating points $p^\star \in [0.80, 0.99]$, with mean test
MTBFA values $0.110$--$0.294$\,h.
Two architectures (LSTM, GRU) require $p^\star = 0.99$ (the boundary of
our sweep), and reach mean test MTBFA of $0.117$\,h and $0.099$\,h
respectively---LSTM is marginally feasible and GRU is marginally
infeasible relative to the $0.10$\,h target. We therefore characterise
both as borderline rather than reliably feasible: their probability
distributions concentrate too heavily near $p=1$ to leave headroom for
calibration robustness.
The default-threshold universal failure is therefore a calibration
artefact for the majority but a genuine capacity limit for the borderline
pair.
MTBFA-feasibility alone, however, is not deployment-readiness:
under the joint criterion (DR@$5\,$s~$\geq 0.85$ \emph{and}
MTBFA~$\geq 0.10$\,h), only CNN-LSTM remains feasible after calibration
(test DR@5\,s~$=0.880$, MTBFA~$=0.178$\,h). The other four
MTBFA-feasible architectures fall below the DR@5\,s floor at their
calibrated operating points (CNN $0.764$, BiLSTM $0.510$, TCN $0.603$,
Transformer $0.490$), so the calibration cost translates directly into
joint-deployability rejection for every architecture except CNN-LSTM.

\textbf{(b) Calibration cost varies by a factor of five across architectures.}
CNN-LSTM achieves operational MTBFA at the smallest DR@5\,s cost
($-0.08$, from $0.96$ to $0.88$);
Transformer pays the largest cost
($-0.43$, from $0.92$ to $0.49$). This factor-of-five difference
is invisible to either conventional precision/recall ranking or the
default-threshold time-aware ranking.

\textbf{(c) The trade-off shape itself is an architectural fingerprint.}
Figure~\ref{fig:pareto-calibration} plots the test-set
$(\text{MTBFA},\,\text{DR@5\,s})$ Pareto curve obtained by sweeping
$p \in [0.50, 0.99]$ for every architecture.
Three qualitatively distinct regimes appear:
(i)~\emph{wide-shouldered} curves (CNN-LSTM, CNN) sustain DR@5\,s
near the in-distribution maximum across one to two orders of magnitude
of MTBFA improvement;
(ii)~\emph{steep-falloff} curves (Transformer, BiLSTM) lose DR@5\,s
sharply as soon as $p$ moves above the default;
(iii)~\emph{wall-bounded} curves (LSTM, GRU) require the maximum
swept threshold to approach the MTBFA target and therefore offer no
further headroom for calibration tightening.
The calibration regime is an architectural property in its own right,
not a hyperparameter to be casually tuned at deployment time.

\paragraph{Deployment selection rule.}
The combination of architecture-dependent calibration cost
(Table~\ref{tab:calibrated-threshold}), trade-off-shape heterogeneity
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto-calibration}), and budget-monotone ordering
(Table~\ref{tab:multi-mtbfa-target}) suggests a three-step protocol
for detector selection in safety-critical UAV deployment:
(1)~for every candidate architecture, generate the validation-set
$(\text{MTBFA},\,\text{DR@}\Delta t)$ Pareto curve over a fine threshold sweep;
(2)~choose the architecture whose Pareto curve dominates at the
mission-specific $(\text{MTBFA}_{\text{target}},\,\text{DR@}\Delta t_{\text{target}})$
operating point;
(3)~validate the chosen operating point on a held-out test set to check
distribution-shift robustness of the chosen $p^\star$.
On the in-distribution test set, this protocol uniquely identifies CNN-LSTM
as the deployable architecture under the primary $(0.85, 0.10\,\text{h})$
operating budget; on the cross-distribution evaluation in
\S\ref{sec:cross_domain}, the same protocol re-orders the candidates
substantially, motivating the cross-distribution analysis as a complementary
deployment-selection input.

At the default operating threshold $p=0.5$, every architecture's
MTBFA falls in the range $0.016$--$0.024$\,h
(Table~\ref{tab:deployability}), well below both the $0.10$\,h
deployability target and a relaxed $0.05$\,h alternative; consequently
the gap between conventional and time-aware deployability is
structural under the uncalibrated operating point and not an
artefact of where the MTBFA threshold is drawn, which is precisely
why the per-architecture threshold calibration of
Table~\ref{tab:multi-mtbfa-target} is required to recover deployable
operating points for any architecture.

\subsection{Cross-Distribution Generalization Evaluation}
\label{sec:cross_domain}

To examine how detectors trained on the MATLAB simulation generalise
beyond the training distribution, we conduct a zero-shot evaluation that
probes sim-to-real transferability and broader cross-distribution
robustness, motivated by the well-documented reality gap in
simulation-trained learners~\cite{ref51} and by the cross-domain
generalization challenges identified in the deep domain-adaptation
literature~\cite{ref52}. We evaluate the 35 trained checkpoints
\emph{without fine-tuning} on three additional data sources:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{IEEE DataPort UAV Attack Dataset (live)}~\cite{ref38},
  containing real Holybro S500 multirotor flights with hardware
  GPS spoofing performed by a HackRF One SDR~\cite{ref48} (1 benign

  windowing). Because the detection task in this study is GPS spoofing,
  the loader retains only the benign and spoofing flights; the jamming
  flight is excluded so that the test distribution does not introduce a
  third anomaly class outside the trained label space.
\item \textbf{IEEE DataPort UAV Attack Dataset (PX4 SITL)}~\cite{ref38},

\item \textbf{ALFA dataset (real fixed-wing)}~\cite{mousaei2024alfa},

The cross-distribution evaluation reveals two patterns:

\textbf{Architecture ranking is not preserved across distributions.}
CNN-LSTM, the highest-scoring architecture on the in-distribution test set
(DR@5\,s = $0.958$), drops to $0.200$ on the live IEEE DataPort hardware data
and to $0.089$ on the multi-airframe SITL data---the largest in-distribution-to-
DataPort drop of all seven architectures.
LSTM and GRU, mid-pack on the in-distribution test set, are the only
architectures whose DataPort-live DR@5\,s exceeds $0.80$.
This rank inversion is robust across two of the three cross-distribution
sources and motivates explicit cross-distribution validation when selecting
detector architectures for deployment.

\textbf{Conventional precision collapses uniformly across all architectures
on the live data.}
Precision falls from the in-distribution range
($\approx 0.79$--$0.90$) to a much narrower
$0.38$--$0.42$ band on IEEE DataPort live, with similar collapse on ALFA
($0.37$--$0.42$). Recall is largely preserved ($0.48$--$0.71$ on DataPort
live), indicating that the architectures over-fit to the simulator's
clean false-positive characteristics: real flight dynamics produce many
true normal samples that the models flag as anomalous.
The implication for deployment is that simulator-trained detectors should
either be calibrated for higher operating thresholds or fine-tuned on a
small real-flight calibration set before deployment---a recommendation we
discuss further in \S\ref{sec:limitations}.

\textbf{Scope of the claim.}
We emphasise that this is a \emph{cross-distribution} rather than a strict
\emph{sim-to-real} evaluation: the three cross-distribution sources differ
from the training distribution along multiple axes (vehicle airframe,
nominal cruise speed, sensor noise statistics, and---for IEEE DataPort
spoofing---attack mechanism). We design a controlled comparison in
\S\ref{sec:alfa-controlled} to disentangle the platform-mismatch contribution
from the broader sim-to-real gap.

\subsection{Controlled Platform-Matching Experiment}
\label{sec:alfa-controlled}

The cross-distribution drop reported in \S\ref{sec:cross_domain} conflates
several confounds (vehicle type, cruise speed, training-set scale,
spoofing-attack mechanism). To isolate the contribution of nominal
platform-speed matching---a commonly hypothesised remedy for sim-to-real
gaps---we run a controlled $2 \times 2$-style experiment specifically
against the ALFA dataset.

We hold the architecture, training pipeline, evaluation protocol, and
random seeds fixed across three regimes:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{5\,m/s 3\,000 flights} (the original power-inspection
  multirotor regime; $1{,}800$/$600$/$600$ split).
\item \textbf{5\,m/s 600 flights} (same speed/altitude, downsized
  training set to $360$/$120$/$120$ to match the matched regime's scale).
\item \textbf{20\,m/s matched 600 flights} (UAV\_SPEED bumped to
  $20$\,m/s and altitude bumped to $120$\,m to nominally match
  ALFA's Carbon-Z fixed-wing cruise regime; same $360$/$120$/$120$
  scale; sensor noise and attack parameters held identical).
\end{enumerate}

Comparing regimes (1) and (2) isolates the effect of training-set scale;
comparing (2) and (3) isolates the effect of nominal platform-speed
matching. Each regime contributes seven trained architectures
($\times 5$ seeds), all evaluated zero-shot against the same ALFA
real-flight set. Table~\ref{tab:alfa-controlled} reports DR@5\,s for
each (architecture, regime) cell; Fig.~\ref{fig:alfa-controlled-bars}
visualises the same data.

The matrix produces three observations.

\textbf{Training-set scale alone explains very little of the gap.}
Comparing $5$\,m/s 3\,000-flight to $5$\,m/s 600-flight, the
worst-affected architecture (TCN) loses $0.32$ on DR@5\,s; the rest
move by at most $\pm 0.12$.
The aggregate $5\times$ reduction in training data is, for most
architectures, a moderate effect.

\textbf{Matching nominal platform speed is a counter-intuitive net negative.}
Comparing $5$\,m/s 600-flight to $20$\,m/s matched 600-flight (same
training-set scale, same mission generator template, only nominal
speed/altitude differ), the matched-platform regime degrades
DR@5\,s by $0.36$--$0.96$ for five of the seven architectures.
CNN drops from $0.96$ to $0.00$, CNN-LSTM from $0.92$ to $0.08$, and
TCN from $0.68$ to $0.16$.
The simpler recurrent architectures (LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU) are less
affected ($0.36$, $0.28$, $0.36$ degradations respectively) but are
still strictly worse under the matched regime.

\textbf{We propose a candidate mechanism (subject to future work).}
At $5$\,m/s, a $15$\,m step attack produces a position-perturbation
signal lasting $\sim 3$\,s before steady state, leaving multiple
sliding windows in which the model can integrate the perturbation.
At $20$\,m/s the same attack settles within $\sim 0.75$\,s---a single
sliding window---and the resulting feature distribution shifts toward
short-burst signatures. Under variable-speed real flight (ALFA includes
take-off, turn, and approach segments where actual speed is below
nominal cruise), the wider-temporal-support features learnt from the
slower simulation may simply transfer more robustly. We do not claim
this mechanism is the unique cause; the empirical observation
(matched-platform speed worsens transfer) is what we report as the
contribution.

\textbf{Implication for simulator-regime selection.}
The observation cautions against treating the standard
``match-the-platform'' heuristic as a one-step sim-to-real-gap remedy:
on this dataset and attack configuration, nominal platform-speed matching
is neither sufficient nor beneficial. We do not claim that platform
matching is universally counterproductive; rather, the result motivates
empirical Pareto-curve evaluation across candidate simulator regimes
(rather than commitment to a single nominally matched regime), and it
suggests that simulator regimes maximising the temporal support of
attack signatures relative to the detector's window length deserve
specific consideration.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Generality of the Framework}

The time-aware evaluation framework is deliberately detector-agnostic and
task-agnostic. Three design choices enable this generality.

\textbf{Decoupling from the sliding-window assumption.}
DR@$\Delta t$, ADD, and MTBFA are defined over sequences of timestamped binary
decisions $\hat{y}_k$ and require only that each decision carries a causal
timestamp and that an event onset is defined.
Detectors issuing per-step decisions (e.g.\ online RNNs, event-triggered
architectures) can be evaluated by the same formulae without modification.

\textbf{Portability to other event-detection domains.}
The three metrics apply to any system that must raise a first alarm within a
budget where repeated false positives are operationally disruptive.
Examples include industrial fault prediction (alarm management standards require
MTBFA $\geq 10\,\text{min}$~\cite{ref56}), medical monitoring, and network
intrusion detection~\cite{ref10}. Adapting the framework requires only
re-grounding the thresholds to the target domain's physical risk model.

\textbf{Case study framing.}
The seven detectors in Section~\ref{sec:casestudy} demonstrate the framework's
discriminative power. Specific metric values will change with training data and
hyperparameters; what remains invariant is the framework's ability to reveal
detectors that pass conventional metrics but fail operational time-aware standards.

\subsection{What the Cross-Distribution Evaluation Tells Us}
\label{sec:cross-discussion}

Sections~\ref{sec:cross_domain} and \ref{sec:alfa-controlled} provide three
findings that, taken together, give actionable deployment guidance even
under the framework-agnostic interpretation.

\textbf{In-distribution architectural ranking does not predict
cross-distribution ranking.}
CNN-LSTM is the in-distribution leader but the largest cross-distribution
loser on two of the three evaluated sources. Practitioners selecting a
detector architecture from in-distribution validation alone risk choosing
the architecture that generalises worst.

\textbf{Simpler recurrent architectures (LSTM/GRU) generalise more
robustly across distributions.}
Across all three cross-distribution sources, LSTM and GRU lose less
DR@5\,s than the larger CNN-LSTM and CNN architectures. This is consistent
with the broader machine-learning observation that lower-capacity models
trained on a single distribution often transfer more robustly because
they are forced to learn more generic, lower-variance features.

\textbf{Naive platform-speed matching is not a remedy.}
The controlled experiment in \S\ref{sec:alfa-controlled} demonstrates
that matching the simulator's nominal cruise speed to the target
platform's nominal cruise speed degrades transfer for every architecture.
The mechanism we propose is that slower-speed simulation produces longer
attack signatures (multiple sliding-window observations per attack)
which support richer features that survive the variable-speed real
flight conditions of true deployment. Practical implication: when
choosing a simulator regime to train against, prefer the regime that
maximises the temporal support of attack signatures, even if it does not
match the deployment platform's nominal cruise.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Cross-distribution vs sim-to-real scope.}
Our cross-distribution evaluation conflates several axes
(vehicle type, cruise speed, sensor noise statistics, attack mechanism).
The controlled \S\ref{sec:alfa-controlled} experiment specifically
isolates the platform-speed-matching axis, but the remaining axes
(true GNSS multipath/ionospheric noise, real RF spoofing vs state-level
injection, real wind/operator dynamics) are jointly varied between sim
and real. A rigorously isolated sim-to-real evaluation would require
matched-platform real-flight data we did not collect. Closing this gap
in future work likely requires sim-to-real techniques such as domain
randomization or higher-fidelity sensor models~\cite{ref51}, together
with unsupervised or few-shot domain-adaptation methods that align
simulator-trained representations to in-flight distributions without
labelled real-attack data~\cite{ref52}.

\textbf{Operating-point selection at deployment time.}
\S\ref{sec:ablation} reports a validation-calibrated operating-point
experiment showing that MTBFA $\ge 0.10$\,h is achievable for five of
the seven architectures (CNN, BiLSTM, CNN-LSTM, TCN, Transformer),
while LSTM and GRU remain borderline at the swept-threshold boundary;
the calibration cost is paid in DR@5\,s, ranging from $0.08$ (CNN-LSTM)
to $0.43$ (Transformer) across the five feasible architectures.
The default decision threshold $p=0.5$ is therefore not deployable; any
production use of these detectors requires a calibration step on a
held-out validation set with operational FA budgets, and the cost of
that calibration should itself be reported alongside in-distribution
metrics. Future work could investigate domain-adaptive calibration
methods that recover the calibration cost without sacrificing
generalization.

\textbf{Simulator sensor model.}
The MATLAB GPS sensor model uses additive Gaussian white noise and does
not include multipath propagation, ionospheric delay, or receiver clock
drift. The cross-distribution gap in \S\ref{sec:cross_domain} is consistent
with the hypothesis that real GPS-noise structure is a major confound,
but disentangling sensor-model effects from the other cross-distribution
axes would require a sensor-realistic simulator we do not have.

\textbf{Threshold sensitivity to mission profile.}
DR@$5\,\text{s}$ and MTBFA thresholds are derived for power-line
inspection at 5\,m/s. Different speeds, safety-distance requirements,
or operator workload budgets require threshold re-derivation; the
full DR@$\{1,5,10,15\}\,\text{s}$ curves and CI bounds in
Table~\ref{tab:overall-timeaware} support this.

\textbf{Sliding-window bias on slow attacks.}
End-point labelling assigns the window label from the final sample.
For slow drift attacks, early windows straddle the onset, delaying the
first positive label and potentially inflating ADD. This is consistent
with real operator experience (slow spoofing is imperceptible for the
first few seconds) but should be noted when comparing across attack types.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presents a comprehensive empirical study of seven representative
deep-learning UAV GPS spoofing detectors evaluated under operational
time-aware metrics (DR@$\Delta t$, ADD, MTBFA with false-alarm event
aggregation), grounded in deployment risk constraints derived from the
power-line inspection mission profile.

The study is exercised on a public $3{,}000$-flight MATLAB UAV Toolbox
simulation dataset with seven architectures, five random seeds, and
three real-world cross-distribution sources (IEEE DataPort live hardware,
IEEE DataPort PX4 SITL, and ALFA fixed-wing). Three findings stand out
for the deployment-engineering audience.

\textbf{First}, the operational deployability of every architecture is
dominated by the choice of decision threshold, not by the architecture's
default-threshold ranking. At $p=0.5$ no architecture meets the
operational MTBFA budget; validation-calibrated thresholding restores
MTBFA-feasibility with margin for five of the seven (CNN, BiLSTM,
CNN-LSTM, TCN, Transformer), while LSTM and GRU remain borderline at
the swept-threshold boundary. Among the feasible architectures, the
DR@5\,s cost varies by a factor of five. Under the joint deployability
criterion (DR@$5\,$s~$\geq 0.85$ \emph{and} MTBFA~$\geq 0.10$\,h), only
CNN-LSTM remains feasible after calibration (DR@5\,s~$=0.880$,
MTBFA~$=0.178$\,h); the other four MTBFA-feasible architectures drop
below the DR@5\,s floor at their calibrated operating points. The shape
of the calibration trade-off curve is itself an architectural fingerprint,
with CNN-LSTM uniquely sustaining DR@5\,s as MTBFA improves and
Transformer/BiLSTM losing DR@5\,s sharply.

\textbf{Second}, attack-averaged metrics mask architecture-specific
failure modes. TCN matches the field on temporally-structured attacks
(Delay/Takeover) but collapses on positional perturbations (Step/Drift).
Per-attack-conditional evaluation is therefore necessary for any
deployment that may face heterogeneous threat profiles.

\textbf{Third}, in-distribution architecture ranking is not preserved
under cross-distribution evaluation: the in-distribution leader CNN-LSTM
becomes a cross-distribution laggard on two of three real-world data
sources, while simpler recurrent architectures (LSTM, GRU) transfer more
robustly. A controlled platform-matching experiment additionally
demonstrates that naively matching the simulator's nominal cruise speed
to the target platform's cruise speed degrades cross-distribution
transfer under this dataset and attack configuration, suggesting that
nominal speed matching alone is insufficient as a sim-to-real-gap
remedy.

Future work will focus on:
(i)~large-scale in-the-wild flight data collection on a matched-platform
real-flight rig to enable a strict sim-to-real comparison;
(ii)~domain-adaptive probability-calibration methods that recover the
calibration cost without sacrificing distribution-shift robustness; and
(iii)~extending the framework to other event-detection domains such as
network intrusion detection and industrial fault diagnosis.
